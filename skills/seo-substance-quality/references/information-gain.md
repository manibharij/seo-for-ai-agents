# Information gain: the test that decides whether a page wins

Read this when a page matches its intent, is technically fine, and still does not rank. The
usual reason is that it adds nothing. Information gain is the question **"does this page contain
anything the results above it do not already contain?"**, and it is the most useful single test
in the Substance system because it is the one that cannot be gamed.

It is also the test that most content processes skip, because answering it requires reading the
competition, and reading the competition is slower than writing another page.

---

## Why length and coverage are not the test

Two proxies get used instead of information gain, and both are wrong in a way that costs money.

**Word count.** There is no length that ranks. The correlation people observe between length and
ranking is mostly that thorough pages tend to be longer, not that long pages tend to be
thorough. Padding a 600-word page to 2,000 words makes it worse: the answer is now harder to
find, and on an assistant surface there is more text to wade through for the same substance.

**Keyword or heading coverage against competitors.** Tools that report "the top 10 pages mention
these 40 terms and you mention 22" are measuring similarity, and similarity is the opposite of
the thing that wins. A page that contains exactly what the other results contain has, by
definition, zero information gain. It has demonstrated that it is a competent summary of the
first page of Google, which is not a reason to rank it.

The honest version of that check is useful only in reverse: if the top results all cover
something and you do not, you may have a **completeness** gap. That is worth knowing, and it is
table stakes rather than an advantage.

---

## How to assess it

1. **Read the top results properly.** Not the titles. Open the pages that actually rank for the
   intent you are targeting and read what they say. Three or four is usually enough to see the
   consensus shape.
2. **Write down the consensus.** What every one of them says, in substance. This is the floor:
   a page missing any of it is incomplete, and matching all of it is not yet a reason to win.
3. **Find what none of them has.** This is the gain, and it is the whole output of the exercise.
4. **Decide honestly whether you have any.** Can this business, specifically, add something the
   consensus lacks? If the answer is no, say so, because the recommendation that follows is not
   "write the page better".

Record the consensus and the gap in the finding, because they are the brief. A finding that says
"needs more depth" is useless; one that says "every ranking result explains the process but none
states the actual cost, and this business can publish real figures" is a page worth writing.

## What genuine information gain looks like

In rough order of how defensible each is, and all of them things a competitor cannot simply
copy next week:

- **Proprietary data.** Your own numbers, aggregated from your own operations, customers or
  product. The strongest form available to most businesses, and the most under-used.
- **First-hand experience.** You actually did the thing, and can say what happened, including
  what went wrong. This is the "Experience" in E-E-A-T and it cannot be researched.
- **Practitioner judgement.** Not what the options are, but which one to pick and when, with
  the trade-offs named. Consensus content lists; experts choose.
- **Specificity where others hedge.** Real prices, real timescales, real limits, real failure
  modes. Most content in most markets is vague because being specific is a commitment.
- **Currency.** Genuinely current information where the field moves and the ranking results are
  stale. Real and legitimate, and the shortest-lived advantage here.
- **Original synthesis.** Connecting things nobody has connected, which is harder than it sounds
  and frequently claimed without being done.
- **Better explanation.** Genuinely clearer than anything available. Real, but hard to assess
  from the inside and easy to overestimate.

What is **not** information gain, however much it feels like work: rewording the consensus,
adding more examples of the same point, a more attractive layout, more headings, an FAQ section
restating the body, or a longer introduction.

---

## When there is no gain available

This is a legitimate and frequent finding, and the pack's value is partly in being willing to
return it.

If a query is comprehensively answered by sites with real authority, and this business has no
data, experience or judgement to add, then there is no page worth writing. The honest options
are:

- **Target narrower demand** where the business does have something specific to say. Usually
  the right answer, and the Demand Analyst can find it.
- **Build the thing worth writing about first.** Run the study, collect the data, do the work.
  That is a business decision with a real cost, and it belongs in front of a human.
- **Accept it and spend the budget elsewhere**, which is often the best return available.

What is never an option: writing the page anyway because it was on a plan. That produces the
thin, derivative content the quality systems exist to catch, and at scale it damages the pages
that were fine.

---

## Information gain and assistant surfaces

The same test governs eligibility on assistant surfaces, for a mechanical reason: a synthesiser
draws from several sources and produces one answer. A page that restates the consensus
contributes nothing distinguishable, so there is no reason to name it. A page with a specific
figure, a first-hand account or a clear judgement gives the engine something only that page can
supply, which is exactly what gets quoted and attributed.

So this is not two pieces of work. Information gain is the thing that earns a ranking and the
thing that earns a citation, which is worth saying to anyone who thinks assistant visibility is
a formatting exercise.
